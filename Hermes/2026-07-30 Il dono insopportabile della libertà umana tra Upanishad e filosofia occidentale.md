## Riassunto

La trascrizione esplora il concetto di libertà umana attraverso le scritture vediche e la filosofia occidentale, sottolineando come solo l'essere umano — a differenza degli dei e dei demoni — abbia il potere di scegliere chi diventare. Sartre e il concetto sanscrito di Purushartha convergono nell'idea che l'uomo sia condannato alla libertà, il che implica la totale responsabilità delle proprie azioni. Il Mahabharata, attraverso il dialogo tra Yudhishthir e Bhishma, ribadisce che il destino non è altro che il riflesso delle azioni passate, e che ogni momento è un'opportunità per riscrivere il proprio karma. La Bhagavad Gita e Camus si incontrano nel sostenere che l'azione stessa è una forma di preghiera e che il significato della vita è ciò che l'uomo sceglie di dargli. La Katha Upanishad descrive l'anima a un bivio tra il piacevole e il buono, ribadendo che questa scelta è esclusivamente umana. Il vero peso dell'esistenza umana non è il dolore, ma il potenziale inespresso e la responsabilità che ogni scelta porta con sé.

## Contenuto

In the Upanishads, it is written that even the gods envy human birth because only a human can choose. A God is bound by perfection. A demon is bound by desire. But a human stands between the two, free to become either. Now, that is the unbearable gift of being human, the fact that we have freedom. Sartre once said that man is condemned to be free, condemned because freedom sounds beautiful. Until you realize it means you cannot blame anyone anymore. Not your past, not Karm, not fate. And every choice you make is a declaration of who you are. In Sanatana and Dharma, they call this Purushart, human effort, human will. Even if destiny exists, it bends for those who act. In the Mahabharata, when Yudhishthir stood alone after the war, crushed under the weight of loss, he was this destiny or my doing. And Bhishma, lying on his bed of arrows, answered, destiny is the name men give to their past deeds, meaning that you write your own fate every moment that you act. And that is why the Rishis never feared karma. They saw it as clay, soft, alive, changing with every hand that touches it. Albert Camus once wrote that the meaning of life is whatever you give it. And maybe unknowingly, he was echoing what the Gita had already said, that your action is your prayer, and effort is your offering to the divine. That is the real burden of being human. Not just pain, not failure, but the potential that you have. Because potential means choice, and choice means weight. The Katha Upanishad says the soul stands at a forked road. One path is praya, the other is shreya. The good. The gods already walk the good. The beasts already follow pleasure. Only you, only humans stand between both, deciding every moment which one you'll become. So the question isn't what's your fate? The question is, what are you choosing right now? Because heaven and hell aren't somewhere else. There are two possibilities within you, and that is the terrifying divine way of being human.
